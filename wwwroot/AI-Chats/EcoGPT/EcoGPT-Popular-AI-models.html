Here's a rundown of popular models you can download and run with Ollama as of June 2026, along with what I can tell you about their use cases. On the sycophancy and data provenance questions, I'll be upfront about what's actually known versus debated.

Top Models on Ollama

Llama 3.1 8B (Meta)

  • What it's for: The all-around default — general Q&A, writing, light coding, summarization, reasoning. Runs on 6 GB VRAM, 111M+ downloads.
  • Sycophancy: Medium. Llama 3.1 benefited from targeted RLHF to reduce sycophantic behavior compared to earlier Llama versions.
  • Trained on stolen data? Meta faces multiple ongoing copyright lawsuits alleging their models were trained on copyrighted books and articles without permission. This is not settled — the outcome is still pending in court.

Llama 3.2 3B (Meta)

  • What it's for: Quick tasks, fast testing, simple chatbot use. The most-downloaded Ollama model overall by install count.
  • Sycophancy: Medium–high. Smaller models with strong instruction-tuning tend to be more agreeable to avoid frustrating users.
  • Trained on stolen data? Same legal context as Llama 3.1 — subject to the same lawsuits.

Phi-4 14B (Microsoft)

  • What it's for: Math, STEM, scientific reasoning, structured problem-solving. Scores 80.4% on the MATH benchmark.
  • Sycophancy: Low–medium. Phi-4 is trained heavily on textbook-quality synthetic data and benchmarks well on factual accuracy, but limited public sycophancy benchmarks exist.
  • Trained on stolen data? Microsoft's training data includes publicly available web content plus curated/synthetic textbook data. They've had fewer public allegations than Meta, but the broader question of web-scraped data applies here too — it's legally ambiguous.

Gemma 4 26B (Google)

  • What it's for: Multimodal vision (image understanding), function-calling, agentic workflows. Best if you need an open model that handles images.
  • Sycophancy: Medium. Google's safety-tuning tends to produce models that are compliant by design, which can tip into sycophancy.
  • Trained on stolen data? Gemma is trained on data Google controls internally (web crawl, YouTube transcripts, etc.). There are fewer public lawsuits directly targeting Gemma, but Google has faced general copyright challenges.

Mistral Small 3.2 24B (Mistral AI)

  • What it's for: Strong multilingual performance, instruction-following, broad reasoning. A great choice if English isn't your primary language.
  • Sycophancy: Low–medium. Mistral models generally receive less RLHF-heavy tuning than Meta or Google models, which tends to reduce sycophantic agreement.
  • Trained on stolen data? Mistral has been transparent about using publicly available web data. Some copyright holders have raised concerns, but Mistral hasn't been hit with the same scale of litigation as Meta.

Qwen 2.5 7B / Qwen 3.6 35B (Alibaba)

  • What it's for: Multilingual tasks (especially Asian languages), coding, general reasoning. The 35B MoE version offers strong performance on consumer hardware (24–48 GB).
  • Sycophancy: Medium–high. Qwen models are heavily tuned for helpfulness and agreeability, especially in conversational use.
  • Trained on stolen data? Qwen is trained on a mix of public web data and proprietary Chinese-language datasets. Data provenance transparency from Alibaba is lower than Western competitors, making independent verification harder.

DeepSeek Coder / DeepSeek-R1 32B (DeepSeek)

  • What it's for: Code generation, debugging, structured reasoning tasks. R1 is a strong reasoning-focused model.
  • Sycophancy: Low. DeepSeek's models, especially R1 with chain-of-thought reasoning, show less sycophantic agreement due to their training methodology.
  • Trained on stolen data? DeepSeek was recently caught up in controversy over data distillation from other AI models, and there are questions about training data sourcing from China. This area is murky — verification is difficult.

On the two harder questions

Sycophancy isn't something most model cards report directly, so these ratings are based on observing model behavior in the community, general tuning approaches, and a few academic papers — not a single standardized benchmark. If this matters deeply for your use case, I'd recommend testing a given model yourself with skeptical prompts to see how it responds.

"Stolen data" is a legal and ethical gray area, not a clear yes/no. Almost every major model is trained on web-scale data that includes copyrighted material. Whether that constitutes "theft" depends on jurisdiction, pending court rulings, and your personal stance on fair use / training data exceptions. The lawsuits against Meta (for Llama) are the most prominent, but virtually no major model is fully cleared on this front.
